What it is like to be a charity, and to be an auditor

Every step both people go through, what they need to know at each one, and what the product currently tells them. Written to be read, walked through together, and used as a test script.

Read this first

Four things are true across both flows

The individual screens are in good shape. What is missing sits between them — and the same four gaps appear in both journeys, which is what makes them worth fixing once rather than seven times.

01

The product never speaks first

There are no notifications anywhere. An auditor is not told an offer arrived — and it expires in 46 hours. A charity is not told an auditor was found, or that their report is ready. Both people have to keep opening the app to find out whether anything happened. This is the single largest gap in the experience, and it affects every waiting step below.

02

Waiting has no expected duration

Vetting says "we will be in touch". A booked audit says nothing about when it will be picked up. A submitted write-up says nothing about when it will be reviewed. Each is honest — none of them has a promised time yet — but a person who does not know whether to wait an hour or a fortnight assumes the worst.

03

A charity's first experience of PICK is a sign-in box

Auditors now get an invitation and a welcome that explains what happens next. Charities get nothing: an admin creates their account, and the first screen they ever see is an unstyled password form. There is no equivalent of the auditor's welcome.

04

Every audit is an island

Neither person can see a second one. A charity cannot tell whether this agency is getting better or worse; an auditor cannot see their own record. The data is all there — the audits, the scores, the dates — and nothing reads across them.

Flow one

The charity

A fundraising director at a mid-sized charity. They have hired an agency to raise money on the street on their behalf, and they want to know whether that agency is behaving. They are not a compliance specialist and they use this product perhaps twice a month.

01

Becoming a customer

Not built

PICK agrees the work commercially, then an admin creates the organisation and the user by hand.

What they are asking
  • What is an audit, actually — what will someone do?
  • What will I get at the end of it?
  • Does my agency know this is happening?
What they can do

Nothing in the product. Everything happens in conversation with PICK.

The gap

There is no invitation, no welcome, no first-run. The charity's first screen is a sign-in form that is explicitly scaffolding. The auditor side solved exactly this problem last week; the client side has not been asked the question.

For Jaz

Should a charity be told which agency shift we are auditing, and should the agency know? The answer changes the whole tone of onboarding — and possibly the product.

02

Getting credits

Manual on purpose /credits

One credit books one audit. PICK adds them and invoices; there is no card payment.

What they are asking
  • How many do I need?
  • What does one cost, and does buying more make it cheaper?
  • Do they expire?
What they can do
  • Read the bundle price list
  • See every movement and the running balance
  • Contact their account manager
Try it

Sign in as client@example.test and open Credits. Twelve bought, four available, and every movement explained.

For Jaz

Credits currently never expire — the type exists and nothing writes it. And the price list shows the same £187.50 per audit whether you buy four or eight, so there is no reason to buy the bigger bundle. Both are open decisions.

03

Booking an audit

Built /book

They choose the kind of fundraising, a three-day window, where, and whether they want audio and video.

What they are asking
  • When can this actually happen?
  • Who will do it, and are they independent of my agency?
  • What is this costing me?
What they can do
  • Pick type, window, place, payment method on the shift
  • Request A/V evidence
  • Spend exactly one credit
The gap

They cannot ask for — or rule out — a particular auditor. The screen for that has a route in the design manifest and an empty folder behind it. The rules underneath are built and tested: a conflict of interest hard-blocks and is not theirs to waive. No charity can reach that rule, or be told why it exists.

Try it

Book one. Then open /book/choose-auditor directly — it 404s. That is the gap.

04

Waiting

Silent /audits

Behind the scenes: the audit is offered to every eligible auditor, one accepts, they prepare, they work the shift, they write it up, PICK reviews it. That can be a fortnight.

What they are asking
  • Has anyone taken it?
  • Is it still happening this week?
  • Do I need to do anything?
What they can do

Open the audits list and read a status. Nothing else.

The gap

This is the longest part of the experience and the product says nothing during it. No email when an auditor is assigned, none when the shift is worked, none when the report is ready. The status words are also ours, not theirs — "in review" means PICK is checking our own auditor's work, which a charity has no way to know.

Try it

As the client, open Audits. Nine audits, eight different states. Ask yourself what each one means without being told.

05

Reading the report

Built /reports/:id

The deliverable. Everything else exists to produce this.

What they are asking
  • Should I be worried?
  • What exactly went wrong, and where?
  • What do I say to the agency on Monday?
What they can do
  • Read the score and the failures
  • Raise a concern with PICK
The gap

It answers the second question and not the first or third. The weighted percentage is the largest thing on the page and the hardest to interpret; the 27 checks that passed are invisible, so a charity cannot tell a thorough audit from a shallow one. This is the page the three redesign directions address.

For Jaz

Should the report show what passed, or only what failed? And is the percentage worth keeping at all — it is weighted, so it is not 27 out of 29, and nobody outside PICK can situate it.

06

Doing something about it

Minimal /complaint

They can tell PICK a finding looks wrong. PICK investigates — the auditor never hears from the charity directly.

What they are asking
  • What if I think this is unfair on my agency?
  • Who looks into it, and when will I hear?
  • Do I have to pay for a re-audit?
What they can do

Raise a concern about the audit, or about a fundraiser's conduct.

The gap

Raising it is built; everything after it is not. There is no acknowledgement, no status, no answer coming back. The charity types into a box and hears nothing. Sending the report to the agency — the most likely next action — is also not offered.

07

Deciding whether it is working

Not built

Three audits in, the charity wants to know whether the agency has improved.

What they are asking
  • Is this better than last time?
  • Is it the same fundraiser every time, or the briefing?
  • Is this worth what I am paying?
What they can do

Open each report separately and compare them in their head.

The gap

Nothing reads across audits. This is the question that decides whether they renew, and the product has no answer to it — although it holds every piece of data needed.

Flow two

The auditor

A self-employed contractor, working shifts around other work. They are outdoors, on a phone, one-handed, often with no signal — and during part of the job they must not be seen using the app at all. Every screen below is read in that condition.

01

Being invited

Built this week /welcome

PICK creates an invitation, and sends the link however they already talk to that person.

What they are asking
  • What is the job, and what does it pay?
  • How much work will there be, and where?
  • Who am I working for — PICK, or the charity?
What they can do
  • Set a password
  • Say where they cover and what they can run
The gap

The welcome screen asks its questions before it answers any of theirs. It explains postcode areas; it never says what the work is or what it pays. A person arriving on a bare link from a WhatsApp message needs the second thing first.

Try it

Sign in as admin@example.test, open Auditors, invite an address, and follow the link it gives you in a private window.

For Jaz

The monitoring and data clauses need to be in the auditor agreement before real people are invited. No code enforces that, and none should — but it gates going live.

02

Waiting to be approved

Honest, but silent

PICK checks identity, right to work, and the methodologies they claimed.

What they are asking
  • How long does this take?
  • Is there anything else you need from me?
  • Will you tell me, or should I keep checking?
What they can do

Nothing. The app has nothing in it until they are approved.

The gap

The screen says we will be in touch, which is true and unfalsifiable. Nothing tells them when they are approved, so the first thing that happens is an offer appearing silently in an app they may have stopped opening.

03

Being offered work

No notification Offers

An audit is offered to everyone eligible. First to accept gets it; the rest are withdrawn.

What they are asking
  • Where is it, when, and can I get there?
  • What does it pay, including travel?
  • How long do I have to decide?
What they can do
  • Accept, or decline with a reason
  • See the area and the pay — never the exact pitch until accepted
The gap

Nobody is told. An offer with 46 hours on it sits in an app the auditor has no reason to open. It is also a race — first to accept wins — so an auditor who checks twice a day is structurally disadvantaged against one who happens to be looking.

Try it

Sign in to the field app as auditor@example.test. One live offer, 46 hours left. Nothing anywhere told you it was there.

For Jaz

Is first-to-accept the right model, or should PICK assign? A race rewards availability over suitability, and the eligibility rules already know who is best placed.

04

Accepting

Conflict step deferred

On acceptance the pay is written down as itemised lines, the exact pitch is revealed, and the offer is withdrawn from everyone else.

What they are asking
  • Am I committed now?
  • What happens if I cannot make it?
  • Do I know anyone at this agency?
What they can do

Accept. There is no cancel afterwards.

The gap

The auditor is never asked to declare a conflict. The database blocks a known one, but a conflict PICK does not know about — a friend on that agency's team — depends on the auditor volunteering it, and nothing invites them to. Independence is the product; this is the moment to ask.

05

Preparing

Built Prep

Twenty-nine checks to learn, as cards, grouped by the moment they belong to. Works offline.

What they are asking
  • What am I actually looking for?
  • Where exactly am I going?
  • Have I done enough?
What they can do
  • Work through the cards and mark them learnt
  • Read the brief on the way, underground
Try it

Open Prep from the home screen. Then turn off the network and reload — it still works. That is deliberate.

06

Arriving

No screen

They travel to the site and find the fundraising team — or do not.

What they are asking
  • Is this the right place?
  • Is that them?
  • Nobody is here — what now?
What they can do

Start the session, which is also how the no-show clock starts.

The gap

There is no arrival step. Starting the session is the only thing to press, and it means both "I am here" and "the audit is running" — two different facts. The 45-minute no-show wait is measured from it, so an auditor who arrives early and waits properly is penalised for pressing it late.

07

Working the shift

Built Session

Roughly 45 minutes watching the team from a distance, then 15 minutes being pitched to as a member of the public.

What they are asking
  • Which part am I in, and how long left?
  • Can I be seen holding this?
  • Did I miss something?
What they can do
  • Watching: tally, note, mark
  • Being pitched to: one discreet marker, nothing else
For Jaz

The stage list is data, not code — your walkthrough of a real shift changes what an auditor is asked for and when, without a release. This is the screen where that matters most.

08

Nobody turned up

Built

After a 45-minute wait the auditor can report it. They are paid in full; the charity gets their credit back.

What they are asking
  • How long do I have to wait?
  • Do I still get paid?
  • Have I done something wrong?
What they can do

Wait out the timer, then report it. Or say the team has arrived.

For Jaz

PICK currently absorbs the auditor's fee when nobody is there and returns the charity's credit. That is implemented but unconfirmed — it is a cost we take on every time.

09

Writing it up

Built Write-up

Every check gets a verdict — pass, fail, or a note — grouped by moment. Submitting moves the audit to PICK for review.

What they are asking
  • How much detail do you want?
  • What if I am not sure?
  • Can I come back to it?
What they can do
  • Answer all 29, saved locally as they go
  • Submit when complete — partial is refused
The gap

Twenty-nine judgements are recalled after the fact, and the screen gives no sense of how long that will take before starting. Nothing on it shows what they captured during the shift, so the tallies and markers they took are not in front of them while they answer.

10

Being asked to redo part of it

No notification

PICK can return specific moments for rework. The auditor sees PICK's instruction — never the charity's words.

What they are asking
  • What exactly is wrong with it?
  • Am I in trouble?
  • Does this affect my pay?
What they can do

Re-answer only the moments PICK unlocked.

The gap

Nobody tells them. The work sits waiting on someone who does not know it is waiting — and the answer to the third question is no, their fee is not clawed back, which is worth saying on the screen rather than leaving them to worry.

11

Getting paid

Built this week Home · Earnings

Five states: with PICK, cleared, in the next run, paid, or being looked into.

What they are asking
  • What am I owed?
  • When does it land?
  • Why is that one taking so long?
What they can do
  • See each audit's payment state and the reference once paid
The gap

The states are honest but none of them carries a date. "In the next payout run" does not say when the next run is — and PICK builds those by hand, so there is no schedule to show. Nothing announces a payment either.

Try it

Field app, Home. Two submitted audits at different stages, one with a payment reference against it.

Design direction

Verdict first, then read down

The pattern we settled on for the report — an answer in three seconds, then the evidence in sequence as you read down — is worth treating as the product's spine rather than one page's layout. It suits how both people actually use this: they arrive with one question, and only some of them want the detail underneath.

It applies wherever the system has a judgement and the content has a real order. Both conditions matter. Forcing a verdict onto a list, or a timeline onto something with no chronology, would be worse than what is there now.

ScreenThe verdictThe sequenceFit
Client report Should I be worried? The eight moments of the encounter Both — the model case
Audit detail (client) Where has this got to? Booked → assigned → worked → reviewed → released Both — strong fit
Auditor write-up How much is left? The same eight moments, in order Both — already half there
Credits ledger How many have I got? Movements over time Both — already close
Ops queue What needs me? Ranked, not chronological Verdict only
Assignment console Who can do this? Six parallel tests, no order Neither — do not force it
The next design pass

Two screens, in this order

The client audit detail first — it is where a charity spends the waiting period, it has a genuine sequence, and it currently says almost nothing. Then the auditor write-up, which already groups by moment and would gain most from showing progress and what was captured during the shift.
